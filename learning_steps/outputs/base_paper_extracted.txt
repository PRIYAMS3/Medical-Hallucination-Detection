EnLeM: ensemble learning-based model 
to detect phishing websites
Most Nilufa Yeasmin1, Md Abu Rumman Refat2, Bikash Chandra Singh3*, Zulfikar Alom4, Zeyar Aung5 and 
Mohammad Azim6 
Abstract 
Phishing involves manipulating individuals into revealing private data, e.g., user IDs, bank details, and passwords. The 
observed surge in fraud is related to increased deception, impersonation, and advanced online attacks. Thus, effective 
phishing detection methods are required to mitigate escalating global phishing threats. Existing methods (e.g., heuris-
tics-based, signature-based, and visual similarity-based methods) attempt to detect phishing sites, and machine learn-
ing (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods are effective in the cybersecurity context in terms of learning from data, 
offering insights, and forecasting. However, independent ML algorithms are limited when handling complex data, 
and DL techniques surpass traditional ML methods in terms of performance but require more data and time. To tackle 
these challenges, we present EnLeM, an ensemble learning model designed specifically for phishing website detec-
tion. EnLeM brings together three well-known machine learning classifiers—decision tree, random forest, and k-near -
est neighbor—using a hard voting mechanism, and further strengthens efficiency with Mutual Information–based 
feature selection. When tested on the UCI phishing dataset, EnLeM delivered strong results, reaching 97.21% accuracy 
and a 97.51% F1-score. Compared to individual ML classifiers, it consistently performed better, and it also proved more 
efficient than deep learning models such as CNN and LSTM. Notably, EnLeM maintained stable accuracy across dif-
ferent feature subsets while cutting execution time by roughly 13%. By striking a balance between accuracy, speed, 
and interpretability, EnLeM stands out as a practical and scalable solution for real-time phishing detection with-
out the heavy resource demands of deep learning approaches.
Keywords Phishing, Univariate feature selection, Machine learning, Ensemble algorithm, Random forest, K-NN,

With widespread Internet access and the ongoing emer -
gence of innovative services, such as web 3.0, most indi -

a wide range of tasks, e.g., shopping and banking, thereby 
minimizing the need for physical queues. However, 
cybercriminals can exploit this trend to obtain funds 
illicitly, and the prevalence of phishing kits simplifies 
this process, enabling those with limited technical exper -
tise to initiate effective phishing campaigns. Phishing, 
which is a social engineering attack, involves scammers 
masquerading as trustworthy sources to extract per -
sonal information by connecting through e-mails, tel -
ephone calls, or text messages to access victims’ financial

accounts. The occurrence of phishing attacks is on the 
rise, with attackers observing victims’ online actions and 
bypassing security measures  [1, 2]. The American Fed -
eral Bureau of Investigation identified phishing as the 
primary cybercrime in 2020, and instances of repeated 
attacks have increased. Monthly phishing attacks surged 
by 65%, spawning 1.5 million sites in 2018 [3], and a nota-
ble breach exposed data for 80 million Anthem Health -
care customers in 2014 [4].
Numerous approaches have been proposed for phish -
ing detection. The predominant methods to identify 
phishing are the heuristics-based approach and the sig -
nature-based method. The heuristics-based approach is a 
conventional means of identifying fraudulent web pages, 
and it is primarily used to identify unauthorized sites [5]. 
Despite its accuracy-enhancing role, the heuristics-
based approach is intricate and costly to implement [6]. 
Similarly, the signature-based method is a critical tool 
for identifying phishing sites using a database contain -
ing blacklisted phishing sites. However, the signature-
based method fails to identify newly generated phishing 
sites [7].
Another method to detect phishing websites involves 
the visual similarity–based approach, which relies on an 
assortment of images, logos, text formatting, and HTML 
tags from a database. However, this method requires 
substantial computational resources to realize effective 
image processing [6].
Machine learning (ML)  can also be used to identify 
phishing websites. Despite the various ML techniques 
employed to distinguish between phished and legitimate 
sites, conventional ML models frequently fall short in 
detecting phishing webpages as effectively as browser 
security indicators. For example, only 23% of these mod -
els rely on webpage content to determine legitimacy [8]. 
In addition, the general population may struggle to dif -
ferentiate between the padlock icon in a browser and the 
actual webpage content, and ML-based detection meth -
ods incur considerable computational costs, and their 
sluggish processing can contribute to increased latency 
when identifying phishing websites [6]. Previous studies 
have highlighted other limitations of contemporary ML 
models, including increased false positives, decreased 
detection rates, and classifier inefficiencies [9, 10]. Many 
traditional ML models also struggle with unstructured 
and streaming data due to the diverse nature of real-
world data, which frequently contain noise, irrelevant 
attributes, redundant features, and anomalies [11].
Other studies have investigated methods to identify 
malicious webpages automatically using deep learning 
(DL) models. However, the performance of DL-based 
classification methods, which is influenced by various 
factors, e.g., the available training data and features, 
varies across different algorithms. Numerous features 
can enhance the performance of DL models; however, 
their slower speed can hinder efficient phishing web -
site detection. In addition, training DL models requires 
substantial data and resources, e.g., multiple GPUs and 
parallel processing to expedite the learning and classifi -
cation phases [12].
Due to the limitations of current models, including 
heuristics-based approaches, signature-based methods, 
visual similarity–based approaches, ML-based meth -
ods, and DL-based methods, in identifying phishing 
websites effectively and efficiently, expecting users to 
differentiate phishing sites from legitimate sites accu -
rately in real-time is impractical. Ensemble learning is a 
common ML technique that utilizes multiple ML mod -
els in a cooperative manner to produce better results. 
Here, by leveraging the strengths of different models 
and compensating for their weaknesses, the overall 
outcome can be improved. Several types of ensemble 
models have been proposed, e.g., bagging, boosting, 
and stacking models. The outcomes are frequently pre -
dicted through a voting mechanism, either hard voting 
or soft voting. Ensemble learning methods have been 
employed successfully in a wide range of fields, e.g., 
medical imaging  [13], social media  [14], finance  [15], 
and cybersecurity [16].
This study introduces a lightweight hard-voting ensem-
ble for phishing website detection that integrates decision 
tree, random forest, and k-nearest neighbor, designed to 
balance accuracy and execution time. Mutual Informa -
tion–based SelectKBest is embedded within  cross-val -
idation to reduce dimensionality without data leakage, 
yielding stable performance while lowering runtime. An 
ablation on the top-k threshold demonstrates robust -
ness to noisy or redundant attributes. A comprehensive 
benchmark against five ML and two DL baselines shows 
that the proposed approach attains competitive accuracy 
and F1 with lower computational cost than DL mod -
els. Implementation details include a transparent tie-
breaking rule for the 1–1–1 case and hyperparameter 
optimization via grid search with 10-fold CV, improving 
reproducibility and fairness of comparison.
The primary contributions of this study are summa -
rized as follows:
1. The proposed EnLeM, a hard-voting ensemble 
that integrates Decision Tree, Random Forest, and 
k-Nearest Neighbor. Unlike earlier ensemble models 
such as Ubing et al. [17] and Toolan et al. [18], which 
primarily improved accuracy without addressing 
computational efficiency, EnLeM jointly optimizes 
accuracy and execution time while maintaining 
robustness.

2  Related work
Phishers attempt to acquire personally identifiable 
information, banking information, credit card details, 
and user passwords. Phishing websites are generated to 
mimic legitimate websites in order to obtain personal 
information from users. For example, cybercriminals 
can gain access to relevant personal data via e-mails, text 
messages, direct messages on social media, or through 
video games.
Many phishing detection methods have been proposed 
previously, including signature-based, visual-based, ML-
based, and DL-based methods.
For example, Salihovic et  al.  [19] used artificial neu -
ral networks, logistic regression, random forest (RF), 
SVM, k-nearest neighbor, and naive Bayes methods to 
detect phishing websites. First, they used the UCI phish -
ing websites dataset, which contains 31 attributes. Then, 
they applied two feature selection methods, i.e., the 
BestFirst+CfsSubsEvaluation and the Ranker+Principal 
Components feature selection optimizer. In an initial 
experiment, they achieved the highest accuracy (97.33%) 
using the RF method. Here, the first optimizer reduced 
10 attributes in the phishing dataset with an average 
reduction in accuracy of 1.53%. In a subsequent experi -
ment, they reduced only a single feature. Here, the accu -
racy of the RF model increased, whereas it decreased by 
0.09% for the SVM method. In addition, the RF method 
obtained the best performance on a spam e-mail dataset.
Yuan et al. [21] proposed a phishing website detection 
method based on features from URLs and webpage links 
using the deep forest model, resulting in a positive rate 
of 98.3% and a false alarm rate of 2.6%. Primarily, they 
employed an effective strategy based on search opera -
tors via search engines to find the phishing targets with 
an accuracy of 93.98%. In addition, Jain and Gupta  [22] 
shortlisted 19 of the most important features and ana -
lyzed several features of phishing web pages. Here, they 
used the SVM, RF, neural network, logistic regression, 
and naive Bayes (NB) methods to classify phishing web -
pages, and they obtained the highest detection accuracy 
of approximately 99% with RF.
Gu et al. [23] introduced an automatic system to detect 
phishing web pages using an NB classifier and analyzing 
the feature URL. They classified using an SVM for ambig-
uous web pages. They demonstrated that their system 
obtains high accuracy with low time costs. In addition, 
Moghimi and Varjani [24] proposed a method that com -
bines SVM and decision tree (DT) models, where each 
model served distinct purposes. For example, the SVM 
was used for training, and the DT model was employed 
to generate the rules used to detect phishing websites tar-
geting the banking field.
In addition, many studies have proposed DL-based 
models for phishing website detection. For example, 
Yerima et al. [20] proposed a CNN-based phishing web -
page detection method. Here, they used the UCI phish -
ing websites dataset of 6,157 genuine and 4,898 phishing 
websites, and they compared their proposed CNN model 
with conventional ML models. The results demonstrated 
that their model achieved the highest accuracy.
Zhang et al. [25] proposed multilayer perceptron neu -
ral networks to detect phishing e-mails, and they evalu -
ated the effectiveness and efficiency of their model. They 
compared the proposed model with different classifica -
tion algorithms, e.g., NN, SVM, DT, and NB algorithms. 
They found that their model obtained the highest accu -
racy and recall values, approximately 95%, and they dem -
onstrated that their model was one of the best in terms of 
detecting phishing e-mails. In addition, Rao and Pais [26] 
proposed a classification model based on a heuristic data-
set. They trained eight ML models, including DT (J48), 
RF, logistic regression, Bayes network, multilayer per -
ceptron, sequential minimal optimization (SMO), Ada -
BoostM1, and SVM models, and they demonstrated that 
the RF algorithm outperformed the other models with

the principal component analysis RF (PCA-RF) method

Aksu et al. [27] studied various traditional ML and DL 
approaches (e.g., the neural networks, SVM, DT, DNN, 
and stacked auto-encoder technique approaches) for the 
phishing detection task. They proved that the DL tech -

accuracy ( 80%).
In addition, Zhang et  al.  [28] proposed a model to 
detect Chinese e-commerce phishing websites based on 
the URL and website content. They included 15 domain-
specific features to detect phishing attacks, and they 
evaluated the websites with four traditional ML models, 
i.e., the SMO, NB, RF , and logistic regression models. The 
experimental results demonstrated that the SMO algo -
rithm obtained the best phishing detection performance, 
with accuracy and F1-score results of 95.83% and 95.58%, 
respectively. However, their experiment targeted a single 
domain of phishing sites, i.e., it was ineffective with non-
Chinese websites.
Khan et  al.  [29] proposed the BDLT-IoMT architec -
ture, which integrates support vector machines (SVM) 
with blockchain technology to secure data processing in 
Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) environments. Their 
approach emphasizes infrastructure security, seamless 
peer-to-peer communication, and data integrity across 
distributed healthcare systems. The model demonstrated 
improved resilience to cyberattacks and optimized 
resource management compared to conventional IoMT 
frameworks. Although healthcare-oriented, this study 
highlights how combining ML techniques with block -
chain-based security can provide useful insights into 
designing lightweight and trustworthy detection systems.
Whittaker et al. [30] proposed a blacklisting technique 
based on known phishing websites, and they extracted 
standard features from their dataset to detect phishing 
sites using an RF classifier. They showed that this RF-
based classifier achieved the best accuracy with noisy live 
phishing data and obtained a false positive rate of less 
than 0.1%. However, this method only works effectively if 
the phishing websites have been published or are visible.
Ubing et  al.  [17] proposed an ensemble learning (EL) 
model based on majority voting to detect phishing web -
sites using feature selection techniques. They compared 
their method with traditional ML models, and the results 
demonstrated that their model outperformed the current 
phishing website detection methods, obtaining accuracy 
and F1-score values of approximately 95.4% and 94.7%, 
respectively. However, their analysis involved some 
important limitations. For example, the authors focused 
only on improving accuracy; other performance metrics 
should be considered to be more balanced.
Toolan et  al.  [18] used the C5.0 DT algorithm and an 
ensemble of other classifiers (k-nearest neighbor, SVMs, 
NB, and linear regression) to classify e-mails as phishing 
or nonphishing. However, they only used 8000 e-mails 
with five features, and their systems classified approxi -
mately 50% of the e-mails as phishing, and the remaining 
50% were classified as legitimate. The average accuracy 
and precision values of the C5.0 model were approxi -
mately 97.15% and 98.56%, respectively, and the average 
ensemble accuracy was 93.68%. They also discussed the 
advantages of recall boosting classifiers, which provided 
better accuracy than the C5.0 and ensemble classifiers.

site detection compared to the existing methods.

3.3  Feature selection
The feature selection process involves selecting a subset 
of relevant features or attributes for various reasons, e.g., 
some features are easier for users to interpret, reduc -
ing training time, avoiding unnecessary attributes, and 
improving data compatibility. Previous studies have uti -
lized a variety of feature selection methods to improve 
model compatibility, including univariate selection, 
recursive feature elimination, and principal component 
analysis.
To prevent data leakage, feature selection was per -
formed independently within each fold of the 10-fold 
cross-validation. In other words, for every training fold, 
Mutual Information–based SelectKBest was fitted only 
on that fold’s training data, and the selected features were 
then applied to the corresponding validation fold. This 
ensures that no information from the validation data 
influenced the choice of features.
In this study, we used the univariate feature selection 
method, which selects the best essential features based 
on univariate statistical tests. Here, we used the Select- k-
Best technique, which selects features according to the k 
highest scores, and different statistical tests can be used 
in this selection method. The univariate test uses the 
mutual information (MI) approach (“ mutual_info_clas-
sif” in the scikit-learn ML library) and selects the top 20 
features from the dataset.
The MI between two random variables is a nonnegative 
value, which measures the dependency between the vari -
ables. The MI value is zero if and only if the two random 
variables are independent; otherwise, the MI value is one.

3.4  Dataset splitting
To split the UCI dataset, we used the k-fold cross-val -
idation technique, which is a strategy to enhance the 
single holdout approach. Rather than splitting a dataset 
into the training and the testing (holdout) sets only once, 
this technique divides the dataset into k subsets and 
applies the holdout approach to each subset k times. The 
steps involved in the k-fold cross-validation process are 
described as follows. 
(1)I(X;Y ) = H(X) − H(X|Y ),
1. Divide the dataset into k folds or k chunks.
2. Create k folds from the dataset. For each fold, con -
struct a model using the other k − 1 folds. Then, we 
assess the model’s performance on the k-th fold by 
predicting the labels (i.e., phishing or legitimate) and 
evaluate the accuracy.
3. This procedure is repeated until all k folds have been 
evaluated using separate test sets.
4. The results are recorded, and the expected average 
accuracy is calculated and given as the test measure 
for the model currently being evaluated.
For each of our classifiers, the k-fold cross-validation was 
considered. Here, we experimented with several k values 
and determined that k = 10 was the best choice for our 
experiment.

The goal is to find a suitable function t such that the

Phishing websites continue to evolve rapidly, often imi-
tating legitimate services and bypassing blacklist-based 
detection. Existing machine learning and deep learning 
models face a trade-off: deep neural networks offer high 
accuracy but are computationally expensive, while tradi -
tional ML classifiers are lightweight but prone to unsta -
ble results or high false positives. The specific problem 
addressed in this work is how to design a lightweight 
ensemble learning model that achieves competitive 
accuracy while reducing execution time and maintain -
ing interpretability. The scope of this study is limited to 
centralized phishing website detection using the pub -
licly available UCI dataset, which contains 11,055 labeled 
URLs. The evaluation considers classical ML and DL 
baselines under consistent experimental settings with 
10-fold cross-validation. Broader topics such as dis -
tributed or federated detection, LLM-driven anomaly 
detection, or deployment in real-world browsers are 
beyond the scope of this paper but are discussed as direc-
tions for future work.
3.5.2  Proposed EnLeM framework
We introduce an approach based on hard voting EL that 
predicts the class labels with the highest sum of the votes 
from the learning models. The proposed EnLeM is an 
extended voting-based EL approach, as shown in Fig. 3.
Although we considered alternative ensemble strat -
egies such as soft voting and weighted voting, hard 
voting was selected for its balance of efficiency and sta -
bility. Preliminary experiments showed that soft voting 
improved accuracy by less than 0.3% while increasing 
inference time by approximately 12%. Weighted voting 
also required probability calibration, which was unreli -
able for k-NN and Decision Tree, leading to unstable 
outputs. Therefore, hard voting was adopted as the most 
practical aggregation rule for EnLeM, ensuring robust 
and interpretable results.

legitimate or a phishing attempt. To address this issue, 
we used a dataset containing 11,055 URLs from the 
UCI phishing website datasets (Sect.  3.1). These include 
examples of legitimate and phishing websites. Then, the 
following preprocessing techniques are employed to 
improve the corresponding performance metrics. 
1. Handling missing values.
2. Data cleaning. We remove outliers and duplicates.
3. Feature engineering. We extract irrelevant features 
and convert categorical labels (“legitimate” and 
“phishing”) to numerical values.
4. Normalization.
5. Dataset splitting (Sect. 3.4).
After preprocessing the dataset, we employ the pro -
posed majority voting-based model for phishing web -
site detection. Typically, the EL technique combines 
multiple models that solve a given problem using 
combined decisions that multiple classifiers provide. 
Essentially, EL is used to improve the performance of 
various tasks, e.g., classification, prediction, and func -
tion approximation tasks. Numerous applications of 
EL include decision-making, optimal feature selection, 
data fusion, incremental learning, nonstationary learn -
ing, and error-correcting  [35]. Dasarathy et  al.  [36] 
were among the first to propose the EL approach in a 
divide-and-conquer fashion, where the feature space 
was partitioned using two or more classifiers.
For example, someone planning to purchase a new 
computer could go to a computer showroom and pur -
chase a machine shown to them by the salesperson. 
However, most people are likely to ask their friends or 
colleagues for advice or go to online portals showing 
relevant reviews. Then, they decide which machine is 
best for their needs. Rather than choosing directly, peo -
ple attempt to gain information prior to purchasing a 
computer. EL resembles people wanting to buy a new 
computer.
In the proposed EnLeM approach, with the training 
dataset (i.e., labeled URLs), three classifiers are created, 
and each individual classifier provides its prediction label 
on the test dataset (i.e., unlabeled URLs). Then, every 
classifier provides a label, i.e., phishing (1) or legitimate 
(0). However, this step functions to identify the label that 
provides the most classifiers and make the final decision.
In the rare case of a complete tie (1–1–1), the final 
decision defaults to the prediction of the Random Forest 
classifier, since it achieved the highest standalone accu -
racy among the base learners in our benchmarking. This 
rule ensures consistent and reproducible outcomes when 
no clear majority exists.

3.5.4  Base classifiers
In the context of the proposed model for phishing website 
detection, we combine three supervised learning meth -
ods due to their several advantages. First, there are many 
known outcomes (i.e., phishing or legitimate) in our data-
set, which are crucial for training the model to recognize 
patterns and make predictions. Second, the combina -
tion of three supervised models can predict outcomes 
accurately based on the familiar patterns present in the 
training data. By learning from labeled examples, they 
can generalize their understanding and make informed 
predictions on new but similar instances. Finally, they are 
more efficient and adjust their parameters to realize bet -
ter optimization performance compared to unsupervised 
learning models. In addition, the combination of these 
techniques provides a more comprehensive solution 
when attempting to detect new phishing websites.
In the following, we summarize the three constituent 
base classifiers (i.e., the DT, RF, and k-NN) in the pro -
posed EnLeM.
(i) Decision tree A DT  [37] is a flowchart constructed 
using an algorithmic approach that identifies the ways to 
split a dataset based on different conditions. With this 
method, it is easy to handle categorical and continuous 
data, the model is easy to read and interpret, and the gen-
erated rules are human-understandable. However, the DT 
method is unstable and incurs high computational costs.

(ii) Random forest Bagging (bootstrap aggregation) is an 
intuitive and straightforward ensemble-based technique 
that provides surprisingly good performance [38]. In this 
approach, multiple instances of the base classifier model 
are used for classification. First, bagging creates multiple 
bootstrap samples from the original dataset, where each 
bootstrap sample works as a new independent dataset. 
The individual classifiers are then trained on the boot -
strapped samples. Finally, the outputs of the individual 
models are combined by averaging or voting to create a 
single output with minor variance.
The RF [39, 40] is an ensemble of DTs that employs a 
greedy method to obtain the best split. This technique 
reduces the variance and overfitting of the model, and it 
provides information about significant features from the 
dataset. However, it is difficult for humans to interrupt, 
and this method exhibits poor performance on imbal -
anced data.
(iii) k -nearest neighbor The k-NN  [41, 42] technique 
selects the k-closest examples from the dataset. It is both 
intuitive and straightforward to understand, and new 
data can be added seamlessly. However, this method does 
not work effectively on large datasets with high dimen -
sions, and it requires feature scaling.

systematic procedure ensured fairness and reproducibil -

3.6  Benchmarking
Motivated by previous studies, we compared the pro -
posed EnLeM to five ML models, i.e., the DT, RF, Ada -
Boost, SVM, and k-NN models, and two DL-based 
models, i.e., the CNN and the LSTM models. If the 
ML-based and DL-based classifiers individually predict 
the labels as phishing or legitimate, then each classifier 
is referred to as a single-view learning model. However, 
individual learning approaches can only make up some 
types of applications. In contrast, multiple classifiers 
produce multiple decisions and can perform class predic-
tions on unlabeled data based on consensus.
Bagging-based EL allows more than two weak mod -
els, aggregates their predictions, and provides excellent 
accuracy; however, this method frequently results in high 
bias and underfitting when not modeled correctly [43]. In 
addition, boosting-based EL is challenging because of its 
high complexity.
3.6.1  Tradition machine learning methods

accuracy with the k -NN technique, 91.74% accuracy 
with the SVM method, and 98.35% accuracy with the

AdaBoost Boosting  [52] is a meta-algorithm that can 
be used to realize model averaging. Bagging is primar -
ily used for classification problems and occasionally for 
regression problems. Boosting creates weak classifiers, 
and the sequence of models is created iteratively. Here, 
each model is trained on a complete dataset, and the 
boosting process attempts to add higher weights to the 
instances that were misclassified by the previous model. 
By taking into account the accuracy score and the weight 
of the output of each model, all sequences of the models 
are combined using voting (for classification) or averag -
ing (for regression) to create the final estimation.
AdaBoost [53] is an ensemble of DTs with only a sin -
gle split (one level). It is easy to program quickly, and it 
can be combined with other models flexibly. However, 
the base classifier is very weak and vulnerable to uniform 
noise.
SVM An SVM  [41] selects the best hyperplane that 
maximizes the separability between classes. Note that 
SVM methods are robust against overfitting, and the 
model can solve any complex problem with a perfect ker-
nel function. However, SVMs are memory-intensive and 
require a longer training time than other models, such as 
DT and NB.
3.6.2  Deep learning methods
In addition to the traditional ML methods, the use of DL 
methods is becoming increasingly prominent for various 
tasks. In the following, we briefly describe two DL meth -
ods that were used in this study for benchmarking, i.e., (i) 
1D-CNN and (ii) LSTM.
Convolutional neural network Artificial neural net -
works (ANN) are actively being developed in a wide 
range of fields. CNNs evolved from ANNs, and the first 
CNN to be similar to contemporary network structures 
was the Neocognitron network [54]. In addition, the first 
real CNN, referred to as the LeNet 5 network, has been 
widely used [55] to recognize handwritten numbers. The 
basic architecture of a CNN classifier is described in the 
literature [56].
The CNN model can also utilize a one-dimensional 
structure (i.e., the 1D-CNN) for a processed dataset [20]. 
Note that a particular segment may derive an informa -
tive feature from the overall dataset so that 1D-CNN 
models work efficiently. Some significant applications of 
1D-CNNs include automatic speech recognition, real-
time electrocardiogram monitoring, vibration-based 
structural damage detection in civil infrastructure, and 
high-power multilevel converters  [57]. In this study, we 
used a 1D-CNN model containing a convolutional layer, 
a pooling layer, and one fully connected layer with an 
activation function. We classified the UCI dataset into 
two classes (phishing or legitimate) using the 1D-CNN. 
In addition, we used the ReLu activation function in the 
CNN model to mitigate the vanishing and exploding gra -
dient problems. We also used another classical nonlinear 
activation function, i.e., the sigmoid activation function, 
which is more efficient when performing training on a 
massive amount of data.
Long short-term memory The LSTM is a type of recur -
rent neural network used in DL that was proposed by 
Hochreiter et  al.  [58] between 1995 and 1997 to handle 
the vanishing gradient problem when introducing con -
stant error carousel units. Unlike standard feedforward 
neural networks, the LSTM method has feedback con -
nections. Some applications of this technique include 
language modeling, handwriting recognition, speech 
recognition, image processing, music generation, and 
anomaly detection. LSTM has several units that are 
similar to cells, an input gate, an output gate, and a for -
get gate. Additional details about the basic architecture 
of the LSTM model can be found in the literature  [59]. 
Although LSTM is primarily used for sequential data, it 
can also be applied in classifying non-sequential tabular 
data [60].

4.3  Experimental results and discussion
A comparative experiment was conducted to determine 
the performance of each classification model. Here, we 
used most common cross-validation technique, i.e., 
k-fold cross-validation (where k = 10 was used in the 
experiment). Cross-validation on a k-fold scale divides 
the dataset into k-folds, with each fold serving as a per -
formance test for the model. To complete this operation, 
k repetitions of the training and testing data are required. 
We divided the data into 10 folds using k-folds cross-val -
idation, and each fold was roughly identical to the other 
folds in the dataset. Each iteration of the trained learning 
scheme was repeated nine times, and the performance 
of the learning approaches was evaluated using the 
remaining fold, i.e., the test set. The learning scheme was 
repeated 10 times on the UCI phishing dataset, and the 
prediction accuracies of the 10 repetitions were averaged 
to obtain the overall prediction results in terms of preci -
sion, recall, F1-score, and accuracy.

achieved 97.21% accuracy and 97.51% F1, outperforming 
ML baselines and offering higher efficiency than CNN/
LSTM. Future work will explore integration with feder -
ated or split learning for privacy-preserving deployment 
and investigate advanced dimensionality reduction (e.g., 
autoencoders) and evaluation on larger, more diverse 
datasets.
